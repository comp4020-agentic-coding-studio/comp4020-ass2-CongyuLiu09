# Heads-up

## The dashboard on the windscreen

**Guiding question:** what changes when the instrument moves from the
dashboard to the windscreen?

```notes
Open by pointing at the actual windscreen in the room if there's a car

```

## Learning objectives

By the end of this week you should be able to:

- **Identify** the two structurally different HUD technologies (fixed
  combiner-glass readouts vs. conformal, eye-tracked AR projection) from a
  photo or description alone
- **Compare** the 1988 Oldsmobile Cutlass Supreme HUD and a modern AR HUD on
  what each is capable of pointing at, not just how each looks
- **Explain why** moving an instrument into the driver's forward view creates
  a new risk (visual clutter over the road scene) even as it removes an old
  one (glance time)
- **Trace** the four-stage pipeline that turns a sensor or navigation event
  into a windscreen graphic
- **Argue**, with a specific example, for where the line sits between a HUD
  graphic that helps and one that has started competing with the road for
  the driver's attention

---

## Opening example: the Mazda Active Driving Display

Most HUD-equipped cars sold today still ship something close to Oldsmobile's
original idea, just sharper. Mazda's Active Driving Display, standard on much
of its current range, projects a simple full-colour readout — road speed,
speed-limit sign recognition, turn-by-turn arrow, adaptive cruise status — low
in the driver's forward view, optically set a couple of metres ahead of the
car.

The driver sees: a small cluster of numbers and one directional arrow, never
overlapping the road itself, always in the same fixed position relative to
the windscreen regardless of what's happening outside it.

---

## Opening example: why this is the baseline case

This is the baseline worth anchoring on before the deck gets more ambitious,
because it is deliberately conservative in exactly the way the Cutlass
Supreme was in 1988:

- it never claims to know where anything *is* — only what a number *is*
- it doesn't move when the road moves
- it costs the driver almost nothing to ignore, because it never grows,
  flashes, or repositions itself without cause

Every more ambitious HUD in this lecture is a version of this same base case
that has started trying to point at the world instead of just reporting a
number. Keep that baseline in mind — it's the control condition for
everything that follows.

---

## Two families of HUD technology

Not all HUDs work the same way, and the difference predicts what each one is
capable of showing:

1. **Combiner-glass HUDs** — a small transparent panel rises from the
   dashboard between the driver and the windscreen; the image reflects off
   it at a fixed angle. Cheap, compact, but the image only looks correct from
   one head position and can't cover much of the driver's view. This is
   essentially the Cutlass Supreme's technology, refined.
2. **Windscreen-projected (waveguide) HUDs** — the windscreen glass itself is
   the optical element, usually with a wedge-shaped interlayer that corrects
   for the glass's curve. This is what large-field-of-view AR systems need,
   because the image has to be able to cover a much wider area of glass to
   stay registered with objects across the driver's view, not just sit in
   one fixed spot.

## The focal-distance problem

A HUD only removes the glance-down cost if the eye doesn't also have to
*refocus*. Human eyes take real time — measured in hundreds of milliseconds —
to shift focus between a near object (a dashboard 70cm away) and a far one
(the road, effectively at optical infinity). Early combiner HUDs solved half
this problem: they moved the image up, but still projected it at a single
fixed virtual distance, often only a couple of metres out.

Modern systems go further by rendering at **two virtual depth planes at
once** — a near plane for status information (speed, cruise-control state)
and a far plane, several metres out, reserved for graphics meant to register
with the actual road (a navigation arrow, a hazard highlight). The two-plane
approach is a direct answer to the single biggest limitation of the
Cutlass-era HUD: everything on it lived at one depth, so nothing on it could
ever convincingly sit "on" the road.

---

## Real-world example: Volvo's HUD as an alerts platform

Volvo's HUD implementations are worth a close look for a reason distinct from
image quality: Volvo treats the HUD as the display surface for its
driver-assistance system, not just for speed. Adaptive cruise control status,
lane-keeping-aid state, and forward-collision warnings all surface through
the same windscreen graphic that shows speed. That decision matters for next
week's topic before we get there — once a HUD is the display for an alert
that represents a *decision the car is making* (following distance, a lane
correction), it stops being purely informational and starts being part of
the negotiation between driver and system that week 9 is about.

---

## Real-world example: Audi's dual-plane AR HUD

Audi's AR HUD (fitted to several current EVs in its range) is a useful
counter-example to Mazda's restraint: it deliberately maximises virtual image
size and distance — Audi states a projected image the size of a large screen,
apparently sitting many metres down the road — specifically so that
navigation arrows can appear to be painted onto the actual lane surface
rather than floating near the windscreen. It's useful for this week because
it's the clearest commercial demonstration of the trade this lecture keeps
returning to: the bigger and further-out the virtual image, the more
convincingly it can register with the real world, and the more of the
driver's forward view it is now occupying to do so.

---

## From reporting to overlaying

This is the conceptual hinge of the week. A dashboard gauge can only ever
refer to itself: a needle at "80" means *your speed is 80*, and that
statement is true regardless of anything outside the car. A conformally
mapped HUD graphic makes a different kind of claim — it says *this specific
lane*, *that specific vehicle*, *this exact turn* — and that claim is only
true if the graphic is drawn in the right place, at the right size, updated
continuously as the car and the driver's head both move.

**Direct comparison:**

| Aspect | Dashboard gauge | Conformal AR-HUD graphic |
|---|---|---|
| What it refers to | Itself only | A specific object or location outside the car |
| Fails how | Shows a wrong number | Points at the wrong thing, or lags what it's pointing at |
| Update dependency | Vehicle state only | Vehicle state **and** driver head/eye position |

## How a HUD alert gets to the windscreen

```notes
Walk through this as a pipeline, not a list — each stage is a place the
system could reasonably decide *not* to escalate to the windscreen, and
that's the point worth dwelling on.
```

| Stage | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Signal arrives | A sensor or navigation system produces an event | Radar detects a closing vehicle; GPS reaches a turn |
| 2. Escalation decision | The system judges whether this event needs windscreen-level attention, or whether the dashboard is sufficient | A low-fuel warning stays on the dashboard; a forward-collision risk escalates |
| 3. Conformal render | The graphic is rendered and mapped to the driver's tracked eye position and the road geometry ahead | The arrow is warped so it appears flat against the actual lane surface |
| 4. Projection | The image is projected via combiner or waveguide onto the windscreen | — |
| 5. Attention shift | The driver's attention moves within their existing view — no head movement required | Eyes stay up; focus may still shift between depth planes |

---

## The escalation decision is a design choice, not a technical inevitability

Stage 2 above is where most of the design judgement in this week's case study
actually lives, and it's easy to skate past it. Every one of these systems
could, in principle, put everything on the windscreen — speed, fuel,
navigation, every telltale a modern dashboard carries. None of them do. The
decision about what *earns* windscreen space is a claim about relative
urgency and relevance to the immediate driving task, made once by a design
team, then experienced thousands of times by drivers who never see the
decision, only its result. Get it wrong in the direction of too little and
the HUD is a novelty. Get it wrong in the direction of too much and you've
built a AR windscreen full of exactly the clutter this lecture keeps warning
about.

## Case study, part 1: the 1988 Oldsmobile Cutlass Supreme

The Cutlass Supreme was the first mass-market car sold with a factory head-up
display, and Oldsmobile did not pretend otherwise: the feature was marketed
explicitly on its lineage from fighter-jet avionics, where HUDs had let
pilots read airspeed and targeting data since the 1950s without looking down
into the cockpit during high-speed manoeuvres where a lost half-second could
be fatal.

**Design intent:** solve exactly one problem — the driver glancing down at
the speedometer — by reflecting a green, monochrome digital speed number off
a small combiner set low in the driver's peripheral view. Nothing else. No
navigation, no warnings, no colour beyond the single green.

---

## Case study, part 2: what the Cutlass Supreme got right and wrong

**Strengths:**

- Solved the single highest-frequency glance in ordinary driving (checking
  speed) with almost no added visual complexity
- Fixed to the car, not the world — so it never needed to track the driver's
  eyes, the road ahead, or anything external, keeping the engineering (and
  the failure modes) simple

**Weaknesses:**

- Fixed to the car, not the world — the same property is also the ceiling:
  the display could never point *at* anything, only report a number
- Monochrome green on glass, mounted low, was genuinely hard to read in
  bright daylight — a limitation combiner HUDs still partly inherit
- Consequence for the driver: real time-off-road savings on one specific
  task, and no capability at all for the harder problem — hazards, lanes,
  turns — that would come decades later

---

## Case study, part 3: the modern AR HUD

**Design intent:** rather than reporting a number, place graphics that
appear to occupy the same space as real objects on the road — a navigation
arrow on the actual lane, a highlight around a pedestrian or stopped vehicle
that the car's sensors have flagged as relevant.

**Strengths:** the referent problem the Cutlass Supreme couldn't touch is
solved — an instruction like "turn here" can be drawn *on* here, and a hazard
can be marked at its actual location rather than described in text the
driver has to translate into a direction to look.

**Weaknesses and consequences:** every one of these graphics competes for
the same limited real estate as the road itself. A wide-FOV AR HUD showing a
navigation arrow, a following-distance marker, and a hazard highlight
simultaneously has moved from *annotating* the driver's view to partially
*occluding* it — and unlike the Cutlass Supreme's single green number, there
is no natural ceiling stopping a manufacturer from adding a fourth graphic,
then a fifth.

---

## Case study synthesis: a suggested improvement

```comment
This slide is doing the thing the lecture keeps asking students to do in
Assessment 2 — proposing a rule, not just describing a tension — so it's
worth modelling explicitly rather than leaving the tension unresolved.
```

The Cutlass Supreme's discipline (one channel, one job) and the modern AR
HUD's capability (conformal mapping to the real world) don't have to trade
off against each other; the missing piece in most current systems is a
**hard cap on simultaneous graphics**, borrowed from the same escalation
logic that keeps most telltales off the windscreen entirely. A workable rule:
never render more than one conformal, road-registered graphic at a time:
if a hazard highlight and a navigation arrow are both warranted at once, the
hazard wins and the arrow waits half a second. The Cutlass Supreme never had
this problem because it never had more than one thing to say. A modern AR
HUD has to build the restraint in on purpose.

---

## Comparison: a dashboard warning vs. its HUD equivalent

| Dimension | Dashboard warning light | Equivalent HUD alert |
|---|---|---|
| Eyes-off-road time | ~0.5–1s glance down and back | Near zero — no head movement |
| Cost to implement | Very low (single LED + icon) | High (combiner/waveguide optics, often eye-tracking) |
| Risk of visual clutter | Confined to instrument cluster, ignorable at a glance | Directly over the road scene — harder to ignore, and that's the danger as much as the benefit |
| Best suited to | Non-urgent, self-referential states (fuel, oil temperature) | Time-critical or spatially specific alerts (collision risk, exact-lane guidance) |

## Exercise: audit an AR HUD for clutter

**Task:** find a manufacturer's own demonstration video or press image of a
current AR HUD system (Mercedes-Benz, BMW, Audi, or another premium system
of your choice) showing more than one graphic on screen at once.

**Inspect:** every distinct graphic element visible in a single frame — count
them, and note which are conformal (registered to something specific outside
the car) versus which are fixed-position status readouts.

**Discuss:**

1. How many distinct graphics appear simultaneously in your chosen frame?
2. Which one, if any, would you cut first under the "one conformal graphic
   at a time" rule proposed on the case-study synthesis slide?
3. Is there a graphic in your frame that a driver could reasonably mistake
   for two different things?
4. Would your answer change for a driver in heavy rain, at night, or on an
   unfamiliar road?

```notes
Sample direction: most manufacturer demo footage is shot in ideal daylight
with a clean road — students who pick a frame like this will often
undercount the clutter risk until pushed on question 4. A good answer to Q2
identifies the status readout (speed, cruise state) as least essential to
cut because it can safely move back to the dashboard, unlike a conformal
hazard highlight which has nowhere else to go and still be useful.
```

## Mini knowledge check

1. What is the one thing a fixed-position combiner HUD (like the Cutlass
   Supreme's) can never do that a conformal AR HUD can?
2. Name one design decision that a two-depth-plane HUD is trying to solve.
3. A HUD is displaying a navigation arrow, a following-distance marker, and a
   speed readout at once. Which one, on the logic of this lecture, is safest
   to leave on-screen if you had to cut two?
4. In one sentence: why does giving a display access to more of the driver's
   forward view increase the designer's responsibility rather than just
   their capability?

```notes
Q1: point at, or register with, a specific external object/location — a
fixed HUD can only report self-referential numbers.
Q2: solving the eye-refocus cost between near status info and far
world-registered graphics, so the eye doesn't have to refocus as far or as
often.
Q3: the speed readout — it's the only one that degrades gracefully back to
a dashboard gauge without losing its function; the other two lose their
entire value if not shown at the moment they're relevant.
Q4: because every added graphic now competes directly with the one view
(the road) a driver cannot afford to lose, not with an ignorable corner of
the dashboard.
```

## Summary

- A HUD's core trade is glance time for optical complexity: it removes the
  cost of looking down, but only if focal distance and clutter are managed
  deliberately
- The real dividing line in HUD history isn't "old vs. new" — it's
  **self-referential** (a number about the car) vs. **conformal**
  (a graphic registered to something specific outside it)
- The Cutlass Supreme's one-job discipline and the modern AR HUD's
  world-registration capability are not opposites; a well-designed system
  needs both
- Escalating an alert to the windscreen is a design decision made once and
  experienced thousands of times — it deserves the scrutiny this lecture has
  given it
- More capable display real estate does not just enable better alerts, it
  raises the cost of a bad one, because it now competes with the road itself

---

## Bridge to week 9

Everything this week has stayed on the reporting side of a line: a HUD tells
the driver where something is, or how fast they're going, but the decision
about what to do stays with the driver. Volvo's City Safety, the case study
for week 9, crosses that line — it's a system that doesn't just display a
following distance, it decides autonomously whether to brake and narrates
that decision to the driver after the fact. The windscreen real estate this
week spent learning to point at the world is about to be asked to explain a
choice the car already made without asking.
