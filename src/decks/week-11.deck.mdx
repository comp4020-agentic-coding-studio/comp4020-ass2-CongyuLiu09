# Week 11

## The same car, different countries

Is a dashboard's "urgent" the same everywhere, or is it a local dialect?

```notes
Open by asking whether anyone in the room has driven both a UK/Australian
(RHD) car and a US/European (LHD) car, or owned a car sold in more than one
market. Most rooms have at least one grey-import or rental story. Use it —
this week runs on real, checkable regulatory and layout differences, not
abstraction.
```

---

## Learning objectives

By the end of this week you should be able to:

- **Identify** at least two dashboard features that are mandatory in one
  regulatory market and optional, delayed, or absent in another
- **Explain** how a rating criterion from a body like Euro NCAP propagates
  into a standard-fit dashboard feature without ever becoming a law
- **Compare** the seatbelt-reminder or speed-limit-recognition alert as
  implemented under Euro NCAP/EU regulation versus US NHTSA regulation
  versus Japanese regulation
- **Explain why** a right-hand-drive and left-hand-drive version of the same
  model are not simply mirror images of each other's scanning demands
- **Argue**, with a stated trade-off, whether a harmonised minimum alert
  vocabulary would help or hurt drivers in at least one specific market

---

## Opening example: one platform, three dashboards

Take a single global model — a mainstream compact SUV built on one platform
and sold, with only modest changes, in Europe, North America and Japan. Pop
the bonnet and the platform is close to identical. Look at the instrument
binnacle instead, and it isn't. The European-market car very likely has a
standard speed-limit-recognition display and an escalating, multi-stage
seatbelt chime for every seat, front and rear. The US-market car very likely
has neither as standard, or has a shorter, less insistent seatbelt chime
that legally only has to run for a matter of seconds. The Japanese-market
car may mandate an entirely different feature — automatic emergency braking
as standard even on the most basic domestic trim — years before that became
a US requirement.

---

## Opening example: what the car is actually saying, and to whom

None of this is the manufacturer being inconsistent. Each version of the
dashboard is answering the question "what is urgent enough to interrupt this
driver?" correctly, for the regulatory environment it was built to satisfy.
The car isn't confused about what matters — the definition of "matters
enough to alert on" was handed to the manufacturer by a different authority
in each market, before a single line of dashboard software was written. That
is the whole premise of this week: urgency on a dashboard is not measured,
it's assigned, and it's assigned locally.

```notes
Land this before moving on: students will want to treat one market's version
as "the real one" and the others as compromises. Push back — each is a
correct answer to a different question, not a degraded answer to the same
one.
```

## Regulation as grammar, not just rules

Three bodies do most of the work in this week's case study, and they are not
interchangeable:

- **Euro NCAP** — a voluntary, ratings-based body. It doesn't write law, but
  a five-star rating is commercially important enough in Europe that its
  scoring criteria function as a de facto mandate for anything sold there
- **US NHTSA** — sets binding Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards
  (FMVSS), and separately runs its own New Car Assessment Program (NCAP)
  rating, but the binding standard and the rating criteria don't always move
  in step, and don't always match Euro NCAP's
- **Japan's regulator (MLIT) and JNCAP** — sets its own type-approval
  requirements and runs its own ratings program, on its own schedule, shaped
  by a market with its own vehicle mix (kei cars are a large share of
  Japanese sales and have their own rules)

A feature can be a hard legal requirement in one of these systems, a
rating-only incentive in a second, and simply not addressed in a third.

---

## Real example: Intelligent Speed Assistance, mandated in the EU

The EU's General Safety Regulation (Regulation 2019/2144) required
Intelligent Speed Assistance — a system that reads speed-limit signs or
digital map data and displays, and can gently push back against, the current
limit — on all new vehicle types from July 2022, extending to all new
vehicles registered from July 2024. This is why a current Volkswagen Golf or
Ford Focus sold in the EU shows a speed-limit icon and a soft warning chime
that a nearly identical Focus or Golf sold in the US does not carry as
standard. The feature is useful for this week specifically because it is a
hard EU legal mandate with no equivalent US federal requirement — the
clearest possible case of the same platform, two vocabularies.

---

## Real example: the seatbelt chime, mandated two different ways

The US federal standard (FMVSS 208) requires a seatbelt warning light and an
audible chime, but historically only for a brief minimum duration — a few
seconds is enough to satisfy the letter of the rule. Euro NCAP, by contrast,
has for years awarded rating points only to seatbelt reminders that escalate
— getting louder, more frequent, or adding a visual pulse the longer the
belt stays unfastened — and, in its more recent protocols, only awards full
points when that escalation covers rear seats too. Manufacturers chasing a
five-star Euro NCAP rating fitted the escalating version across whole
European ranges well before any EU law forced the point. The US-market
version of the same model can legally ship with the minimum chime instead,
and often has.

---

## Comparison within the core content: Euro-spec vs US-spec, same model

| Aspect | Euro-spec dashboard | US-spec dashboard |
|---|---|---|
| Speed-limit recognition | Standard, EU-mandated since 2024 | Not federally required; often absent or a paid option |
| Seatbelt chime | Escalating, often covers all seats | Legally sufficient after a few seconds; may not escalate |
| Driver behind the wheel | Sees continuous ambient reminders of both limit and belt status | Sees a shorter, more easily satisfied version of the same category of alert |
| Why the gap exists | Euro NCAP scoring pressure plus a binding EU mandate | No equivalent federal mandate for speed-limit display; a weaker minimum chime requirement |

Same platform, same underlying risk, two different answers to "how insistent
should this be."

## The process: from a rating criterion to a dashboard icon

The gap between markets isn't random — it follows a repeatable chain:

1. **A safety body sets a criterion** — Euro NCAP adds a scoring point for
   escalating seatbelt reminders; the EU General Safety Regulation mandates
   Intelligent Speed Assistance outright
2. **A manufacturer decides standard vs optional, per market** — fit it as
   standard where the rating or law demands it, leave it optional or absent
   where neither body has asked for it
3. **The implementation is localised** — icon conventions, spoken or
   written language for any voice/text component, and RHD/LHD instrument
   layout are all adapted for that specific market's build
4. **The same platform ships with a different alert vocabulary** — a Golf
   built in the same factory carries a materially different dashboard
   depending on the country it's destined for

Steps 1–2 are regulatory and commercial decisions made long before step 4's
dashboard exists.

## Real example: Japan's own schedule, Toyota and Honda

Japan's path doesn't track Europe's or the US's. Japanese manufacturers and
MLIT moved to make automatic emergency braking (AEB) standard on new
kei-cars and many mainstream domestic models well ahead of equivalent US
federal action — an industry-government push through the early 2020s aimed
specifically at Japan's older-driver demographic and dense low-speed urban
driving. A domestic-market Toyota or Honda kei car can carry AEB and its
associated dashboard warning as standard while a similarly positioned
small car sold new in the US, at the same time, did not have to. This
matters for the week because it shows the divergence isn't simply "Europe
strict, US lenient, Japan somewhere in between" — each market leads on the
feature that answers its own most pressing risk profile.

---

## Real example: Tesla and the software-defined shortcut

Tesla is useful here for the opposite reason: because its dashboard is
software-defined rather than fixed at the factory, Tesla can localise an
alert's threshold, language or presence with an over-the-air update rather
than a hardware or trim change. Where a conventional manufacturer's
market-by-market divergence is baked in at production, Tesla's is, in
principle, adjustable after the car is already on the road. That doesn't
remove the underlying regulatory divergence — Tesla still has to satisfy
each market's requirements separately — but it changes *how* the divergence
gets implemented, and it's the thread this week hands forward to week 12.

## Layout is a language too — and it isn't just mirrored

It's tempting to assume a right-hand-drive car is simply a left-hand-drive
car flipped left-to-right: move the wheel, mirror the cluster, done. The
instrument binnacle position relative to the door pillar does mirror. What
doesn't automatically come along for the ride is the driver's *scanning
habit* — the learned, practiced sequence of glances between road, cluster,
mirrors and blind spot, built over years around one specific geometry and
one specific side of the road to watch for oncoming traffic and merging
vehicles.

## Real example: the indicator stalk that didn't mirror

A concrete, well-documented case of layout not simply mirroring: grey-market
right-hand-drive conversions of American pickup trucks for Australia — Ford
F-150 and Ram 1500 conversions carried out by firms such as RMA Automotive
and Walkinshaw — have been reported by Australian motoring press to leave
the indicator stalk on the left-hand-drive-convention side, opposite the
side Australian drivers have used their whole driving lives, because fully
re-engineering the steering column is costly. The wheel and cluster moved.
The habit-critical stalk position, on some of these conversions, didn't
follow — and drivers used to Japanese- or European-convention RHD cars
notice immediately.

---

## Real example: Mazda and the JDM/UK stalk convention

The same point shows up even without a conversion involved. Japanese
domestic-market cars, including Mazda's, commonly place the indicator stalk
on the right and the wiper stalk on the left, matching the steering wheel
side. British-built or European-sourced right-hand-drive cars sold in the
UK, by contrast, typically keep the indicator on the left stalk, unchanged
from the left-hand-drive layout, purely moving the wheel and pedals. Two
genuinely different RHD conventions exist side by side, both long-
established, and a driver moving between a JDM import and a UK-market car
of the same nominal handedness still has to relearn which stalk does what.

---

## Case study: the same telltale, tuned three ways

Bring the regulatory and layout threads together on one alert pair —
speed-limit recognition and seatbelt reminders — across the three markets
this week has been building toward. Euro NCAP's rating pressure plus the EU
General Safety Regulation gave Europe a standard, escalating, all-seats
version of both. The US federal minimum gave American drivers a legally
sufficient but far less insistent version of each, with no federal push
behind speed-limit display specifically. Japan's own schedule prioritised a
different feature entirely — AEB as standard, ahead of either — reflecting
a domestic risk profile built around an ageing driver population and dense
low-speed streets rather than the motorway-speed risk profile Euro NCAP's
criteria were largely built to address.

---

## Case study: strengths, weaknesses, consequences for the driver

The European approach's strength is consistency — a driver who learns the
escalating chime once can trust it will behave the same way in almost any
new European car. Its weakness is that "more insistent, in every seat" has a
real cost in driver annoyance, which is exactly the alarm-fatigue trade-off
week 10 raised. The US approach's strength is that manufacturers retain more
freedom to tune insistence to what a specific model's buyers tolerate; its
weakness is that "legally sufficient" and "actually effective" are not the
same claim, and the gap is measurable in seatbelt-usage statistics between
regions. Japan's approach shows a third strength — leading on the feature
that matches the market's actual risk — at the cost of a driver moving
between Japan and Europe finding neither market's "most urgent" feature
familiar.

---

## Case study: a suggested synthesis

None of the three approaches is simply correct. A plausible synthesis, and
one worth arguing with rather than accepting outright, is a harmonised
*minimum* alert vocabulary — a small, shared core (a small number of
universally red-tier alerts, a shared minimum seatbelt-chime escalation
curve) that every market's regulator agrees to, layered underneath whatever
market-specific features each region's risk profile and rating body still
add on top. That would mean a driver's most safety-critical alerts transfer
between countries even while everything above that floor stays locally
tuned — trading some of each market's autonomy for a driver's ability to
carry urgency literacy across a border.

---

## Comparison: the same alert, three regulatory regimes

| Feature | Euro NCAP / EU | US NHTSA | Japan (MLIT / JNCAP) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speed-limit recognition display | Mandated for all new vehicles from July 2024 (GSR) | No federal mandate; rating incentive only, inconsistently applied | No equivalent nationwide mandate at the same point |
| Seatbelt reminder | Escalating, rating-driven, increasingly covers rear seats | Legally required but minimum duration can be brief (FMVSS 208) | Required, on a separate domestic schedule and standard |
| Automatic emergency braking | Rating-driven, widely standard by mid-2020s | Federal mandate (FMVSS 127) targeted for 2029 | Industry-government push made it standard on many domestic models earlier |
| Driving side / cluster layout | LHD across almost all EU markets | LHD | RHD |

The table's real point isn't any single cell — it's that no column is
strictly "ahead." Each regime leads somewhere and lags somewhere else.

## Classroom exercise: build your own three-market comparison

Pick one dashboard alert this course hasn't already used as its running
example — a tyre-pressure telltale, a driver-drowsiness warning, a blind-
spot indicator, anything with a plausible regulatory story. Working in
small groups, research and answer:

1. Is this alert mandated by law, incentivised by a rating body, or neither,
   in the EU, the US, and Japan?
2. Where the requirement differs, is the gap regulatory, commercial, or a
   naming/localisation difference dressed up as a bigger gap than it is?
3. If you had to pick one market's version as the floor for a harmonised
   minimum, which would you pick, and what would the other two markets lose?
4. Name one RHD/LHD layout consequence, beyond mirroring, that this alert's
   on-screen position would need to account for.

```notes
Sample direction: tyre-pressure monitoring is a strong pick — mandated in
the US (TREAD Act, from 2007) well before it became mandatory in the EU
(2014) or Japan, so this exercise usefully flips the "Europe always leads"
assumption groups will have absorbed from the rest of the week. Push groups
that pick a purely cosmetic or naming difference to find a second, more
substantive alert instead.
```

## Summary

- A dashboard's alert vocabulary is not a fixed, universal fact about a
  vehicle — it's assigned market by market, by regulation, rating pressure
  and convention
- Euro NCAP, US NHTSA and Japan's MLIT/JNCAP don't just enforce different
  minimums, they operate through different mechanisms — binding law, rating
  incentive, and domestic industry-government agreement — and don't move on
  the same schedule
- A single platform can ship with genuinely different standard-fit alerts
  depending on destination market, before a single unit is built
- Right-hand-drive and left-hand-drive versions of the same model mirror the
  instrument layout but not automatically the driver's scanning habit or
  every regional switchgear convention
- A harmonised minimum alert vocabulary is a real, arguable proposal, not a
  settled answer — it trades local autonomy for cross-border urgency
  literacy

## Bridge to week 12: what would you actually keep?

This week's whole argument is that almost nothing about a dashboard's
current vocabulary is inevitable — not which alerts exist, not how urgent
they're allowed to be, not where the cluster sits relative to the driver.
Regulation, rating pressure, historical accident, and a century of
convention decided all of it, market by market. Week 12 asks the question
that follows directly from that: if none of this vocabulary is a fixed fact,
and a software-defined dashboard like Tesla's or Rivian's can now be
redesigned and re-shipped after the car is already sold, what would you
actually keep if you were designing this vocabulary today, with no legacy
instrument cluster to imitate at all?
