## The brief

> Design a complete, coherent alert vocabulary for a vehicle that does not
> yet have one — and defend it as a system a driver could learn to trust.

Choose your own vehicle and context: a small EV, a motorcycle, a delivery
van, a low-speed autonomous shuttle, whatever gives you a real set of
constraints to design against. The scope should be realistic for the
semester that produced it — twelve to twenty alerts, covering the full range
from routine status through to the ones that mean stop the vehicle now, is a
reasonable target. Twice that is not more impressive; it is usually a sign
the scope wasn't chosen carefully.

Every earlier assessment asked you to work on one alert, or one dashboard
that already existed. This one asks you to build the whole vocabulary from
nothing, which means the interesting decisions are the ones between alerts:
how loud is loud enough for the worst case without being oppressive for the
routine one, which alerts share a channel and which need their own, and what
happens when two things go wrong at once. A vocabulary is not a list of
words; it's the relationships between them.

## What you submit

- a short statement of the vehicle, its driver, and the operating context
  (100–200 words) — enough to judge fit, not a design-fiction short story
- the full alert vocabulary: every alert, its trigger, its channel(s), and
  its place in the system's overall urgency hierarchy, presented so a reader
  can see the whole system at a glance (a table, a diagram, or both)
- a written defence (1,000–1,500 words) that argues for the system as a
  whole and directly addresses at least one failure mode — what happens when
  this system is wrong, and why the design still holds up
- this is a capstone: it should draw visibly on material from across the
  semester, not just the weeks nearest the vehicle type you picked
