## The brief

> Take one dashboard you can actually look at — your own car, a family
> member's, a manual, or a well-documented reference vehicle — and catalogue
> every way it talks to its driver.

By week 4 you have covered needles, lights and icons: three different
grammars for saying "something is true right now." Most dashboards mix all
three without much apparent plan, because they were designed in stages, by
different suppliers, years apart. This assessment asks you to make that
history visible.

A strong audit is not a spec sheet. Naming every warning light in the owner's
manual is the easy half. The harder half is judging whether the vocabulary
this vehicle uses is internally consistent: does red always mean the same
thing, does a chime always outrank a light, does an icon ever contradict what
the equivalent text message would have said? Vehicles fail this test more
often than manufacturers would like.

## What you submit

A written audit (1,200–1,800 words, or an equivalent structured document) with:

- a table or catalogue of every alert on the chosen dashboard: gauge, light,
  icon, chime, or message, with its channel and its assumed audience
  knowledge
- at least one specific inconsistency, named and explained — not "the
  dashboard could be clearer" but "the low-tyre-pressure icon and the
  low-tyre-pressure chime disagree about how urgent this is"
- a photo, screenshot, or clearly labelled diagram of the dashboard you
  audited, so a marker can check your catalogue against the real thing

Declare which vehicle you used and how you accessed it (in person, owner's
manual, manufacturer documentation) in a one-line note at the top.
