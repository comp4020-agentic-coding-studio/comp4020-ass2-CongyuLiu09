## The brief

> Pick one dashboard alert that a real vehicle currently signals badly, and
> redesign it — properly, across every channel it uses — so a driver under
> time pressure understands it faster and more correctly.

"Badly" needs a definition, and defining it is part of the work: an alert can
fail by being ambiguous (the check-engine light), by matching the wrong
channel to the wrong urgency (a chime for something that isn't urgent), or by
assuming knowledge most drivers don't have (an icon nobody outside the
service department has ever correctly identified). Pick a real, specific
failure. "Dashboards are confusing" is not a brief; "the 2015–2019 [model]'s
brake-pad-wear icon is visually identical to its parking-brake icon" is.

Redesigning one channel is the easy version of this brief. The alert you
choose almost certainly speaks through more than one channel already — a
light plus a chime, an icon plus a message — and a redesign that only touches
the icon while leaving an inconsistent chime in place hasn't actually fixed
anything. Treat the alert as a small system.

## What you submit

- a one-page statement of the original alert and what specifically is wrong
  with it, referencing how it currently appears (screenshot, photo, or an
  owner's-manual excerpt)
- the redesigned alert, specified across every channel it uses: what the icon
  looks like (sketch, description, or mockup), what colour it takes and when
  that colour changes, what sound it makes (described precisely enough that
  someone could implement it — pitch, rhythm, repetition), and any text
  wording
- a written rationale (600–900 words) that argues for the redesign using
  material from weeks 1–8 by name — cite the week and the concept, not just
  the vibe of the argument
