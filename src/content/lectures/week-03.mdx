A gauge tells you a number and lets you watch it move. A light tells you one
bit: attend, or don't. Somewhere between those two is a decision someone made
about what a driver could be trusted to read, and how much of their attention
a given fact deserved. This week asks what that decision costs, using the
clearest case in the whole history of the dashboard: the US industry's
mid-century swap of oil-pressure and ammeter gauges for a single warning
light, and its modern descendant, the check-engine light.

## Reading a gauge is a skill, and skills fail under pressure

A continuous gauge assumes the driver is monitoring — glancing at a needle's
position relative to a normal range, catching a slow drift before it becomes
a failure. That assumption held better in theory than in practice. Internal
industry research through the 1950s found that a large share of drivers
either didn't look at their oil-pressure or ammeter gauges at all, or looked
without knowing what a "normal" position was, which meant a genuinely
informative instrument was doing no better than furniture. A gauge nobody
reads correctly isn't conveying continuous information — it's conveying
nothing, at the cost of dashboard space and manufacturing dollars.

## Collapsing a range to a threshold is a design act, not a simplification

Replacing that gauge with a light didn't just make the same information
smaller. It replaced a range with a threshold: someone decided the exact
pressure value at which oil pressure stops being "fine" and starts being
"attend now," and everything on the fine side of that line disappears. The
driver loses the ability to notice a slow decline — pressure easing down over
months — because a threshold light is silent right up until the moment it
isn't. What's gained is unambiguous urgency: no interpretation required, no
skill assumed, no missed reading of a needle sitting in an unfamiliar zone.

## Case study: the idiot light and its modern descendant

By the mid-1950s, US manufacturers were replacing standalone oil-pressure and
ammeter (charge) gauges with a red telltale wired to a simple pressure or
voltage switch — on below threshold, off above it. Mechanics and some
motoring writers of the period called it the "idiot light," partly out of
contempt for the simplification and partly because manufacturers had
concluded, not unreasonably, that most drivers were already treating the
gauge like one. The light was cheaper to build, impossible to misread, and
gave up the one thing a gauge offered: warning before the threshold, not at
it. An oil-pressure light coming on at speed can mean the engine has seconds
of useful life left, with no gauge history to say whether this was sudden or
overdue.

The check-engine light is the same idea pushed to its most ambiguous
extreme. Where the idiot light collapsed one continuous value to one binary
state, the check-engine light collapses hundreds of possible underlying
faults — a loose fuel cap, a failing catalytic converter, a misfiring
cylinder — into the same single amber icon. It solves the readability problem
completely: nobody misreads it. It solves nothing else: the driver knows only
that something, somewhere, needs a diagnostic scan, with no way to tell
"pull over now" from "get this looked at eventually" from the dashboard
itself.
