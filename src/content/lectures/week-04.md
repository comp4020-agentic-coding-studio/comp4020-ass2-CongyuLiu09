Last week ended with the idiot light: one bulb, one meaning, no gradation.
That solved the problem of getting a driver's attention, but it created a
new one. A red light behind a wheel-shaped symbol, a light behind a
thermometer shape, a light behind an exclamation mark in a tyre — each is a
different icon standing in for a different fault, and none of them is
self-explanatory the first time you see it. This week asks the question the
idiot light ducked: how does a symbol earn a meaning that a driver can
recognise without having read the manual?

## An icon is a bet, not a translation

A pictogram is often described as if it were a universal translation of a
warning into a language-free picture. It isn't. A cross-section of a tyre
with an exclamation mark inside it does not obviously mean "one of your
tyres is underinflated" to someone who has never been told that. It is a bet
that a driver will learn the association once, remember it, and recognise it
under stress in a different car years later. That bet only pays off if
essentially every manufacturer is making the same bet with the same symbol —
otherwise the driver has memorised four different icons for four different
brands and forgotten which is which by the time it matters.

## Convergence has to be engineered

Shared symbols do not emerge from good taste. Left alone, manufacturers
draw the same warning in incompatible ways, because each one is solving
their own styling and legal problems, not the driver's recognition problem.
A shared icon vocabulary appears only when something forces competitors to
stop differentiating on a feature drivers need to recognise instantly
regardless of which car they're in.

## Case study: the tyre-pressure icon

Before 2000, a low-tyre-pressure warning, where it existed at all, was
whatever the manufacturer's design team drew: some used a simple "!" in a
circle indistinguishable from a dozen other generic faults, others used a
wheel-and-tyre side profile, others buried the warning as text in a
multi-function display with no icon at all. There was no shared visual
grammar, because there was no shared requirement to have tyre-pressure
monitoring in the first place.

That changed after the Ford Explorer/Firestone rollover controversy of the
late 1990s, in which tread separation on under-inflated tyres was linked to
a wave of fatal rollovers. The US TREAD Act (2000) responded by mandating
tyre-pressure monitoring systems (TPMS) on new vehicles sold in the US,
phased in through the mid-2000s. Once TPMS was legally required rather than
a premium option, every manufacturer needed a dashboard symbol for the same
new function, at the same time, for a regulator that wanted the meaning to
be checkable. The industry converged on the ISO 2575 pictogram — a
cross-section of a tyre with an exclamation mark — within roughly a decade,
and it now appears, essentially unchanged, on cars from every major
manufacturer sold anywhere the ISO standard is referenced. It is one of the
cleanest examples in the whole vocabulary of a symbol becoming standard
through regulatory pressure rather than organic taste convergence: the
mandate came first, and the shared icon was the industry's fastest way to
comply without each firm bearing the cost of teaching drivers a
brand-specific symbol from scratch.
