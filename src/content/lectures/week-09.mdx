Every alert this course has studied so far, from the oil-pressure idiot light
to a heads-up navigation arrow, has done the same job: describe a fact about
the world and leave the response to the driver. The fuel is low. The tyre is
soft. The lane markings say you're drifting. This week's alerts break that
pattern. When a forward-collision warning fires at the same instant the car
applies its own brakes, the dashboard is no longer describing a symptom — it
is narrating a decision the car is making, or has already made, on the
driver's behalf. The guiding question: when the car starts giving advice
instead of just reporting state, what is the alert actually claiming?

## Two different sentences, wearing the same red light

"Your oil pressure is low" and "I am braking" can share the same red
telltale, the same urgent tone, the same half-second of attention — but they
are grammatically different claims. The first describes a condition that
exists whether or not the driver acts; ignore it and the oil pressure stays
exactly as low. The second describes an action the car itself is already
taking, regardless of what the driver decides. A driver trained by eight
weeks of dashboards that describe the world is not automatically equipped to
read one that describes the car's own behaviour instead.

## The warning can arrive with the intervention, not before it

Every earlier alert type in this course preserves a sequence: the car
detects a condition, tells the driver, and the driver has seconds to act
before anything else happens. An almost-autonomous alert can collapse that
sequence. At short following distances there isn't time to warn and then
brake, so the system warns and brakes together, or brakes first and lets the
warning explain what already happened. That is a physical constraint of
time-to-collision, not a design failure — but it asks the driver to interpret
an alert that may describe the past rather than offer a choice about the
future.

## Case study: Volvo City Safety, 2008

Volvo introduced City Safety on the first-generation XC60 in 2008: a
low-speed system, originally active below roughly 30 km/h, meant to prevent
or soften the most common urban collision, a nose-to-tail shunt in slow
traffic. A forward-facing sensor tracks the vehicle ahead; if a collision
looks imminent and the driver hasn't braked, the system brakes for them. Its
interface set the template for the whole category: where there's enough
time, the driver gets an audible and visual warning before the car
intervenes; where there isn't, the system skips straight to braking, because
a warning arriving after the braking has already started isn't warning
anyone of anything they can still change. That staging is the case study's
real lesson and its real weakness. A driver who feels the car brake hard with
no preceding cue has no way to tell, in the moment, whether it just corrected
a hazard they never saw or whether something has gone wrong with the car
itself — and the alert that would resolve that ambiguity is exactly the one
the shortest-range cases don't have time to give. A workable synthesis keeps
the staged warn-then-brake logic wherever the time budget allows it, but adds
a distinct, sustained visual cue timed to the onset of any autonomous
braking, so even a driver with zero warning time can retroactively read "the
car just intervened" instead of "the car just malfunctioned."
