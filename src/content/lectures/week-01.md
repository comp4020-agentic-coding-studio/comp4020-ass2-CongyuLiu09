Start with a question rather than a definition: when a dashboard shows you
something, who decided what counts as worth showing, and how much of your
attention it should cost? A fuel gauge and a check-engine light are both
"information," but not the same kind, and a dashboard that treated them
identically would be a bad dashboard. This course studies that difference —
call it a grammar — across a century of instrument design. Week 1 sets the
terms the rest of the course will use.

## A dashboard is an argument, not an inventory

An analogue speedometer needle and a digital speed readout carry the same
number but make different claims. The needle argues by position — its angle
relative to redline is legible at a glance, without reading digits. The digital
number argues by precision — exact, but requiring a half-second of literacy
the needle doesn't. Neither is more "true." Each is a choice about what the
driver needs under a specific kind of pressure: glance-legibility versus
exactness. That choice is the language.

## Constraint is the whole design problem

A dashboard's audience is a driver who is, by definition, mostly looking
somewhere else. Every element competes for a few hundred milliseconds of
peripheral or foveal attention, at speed, often at night, sometimes while
frightened. A museum label can ask for sustained reading; a dashboard cannot.
This is why the course is not a styling exercise — the interesting decisions
are what gets a needle, what gets a light, what gets silence.

## Case study: Jaguar E-Type (1961) vs Mercedes-Benz MBUX Hyperscreen (2021)

The Series 1 E-Type's dashboard is two large matching circular dials —
speedometer and tachometer — flanked by smaller gauges for oil pressure,
water temperature, fuel and battery condition, all analogue, all continuous
needles, set in engine-turned aluminium. There is no telltale light bank at
all in the earliest cars: a fault shows up as a needle drifting into a
marked danger zone, not as a discrete on/off signal. The driver's job is
continuous monitoring — reading trends, not waiting for alarms.

The MBUX Hyperscreen, by contrast, is a single curved glass panel spanning
the width of the car, housing three displays (driver cluster, centre, and
front-passenger screen) behind one seamless surface. Almost every reading —
speed, range, climate, navigation, drive mode — is a software-rendered
surface that can be reconfigured, restyled or replaced by an update; the
instrument a Hyperscreen driver reads on Tuesday may not be laid out the same
way on Wednesday.

Same four questions — how fast, how much fuel, how hot, which way — answered
by opposite grammars: one fixed, mechanical and non-negotiable; the other
soft, configurable, mediated by software that can change the answer's shape
without changing the car underneath it. Neither is free: the E-Type cannot
warn you of anything its designers didn't wire a gauge for; the Hyperscreen
can show you almost anything, which means someone has to keep deciding what
it shouldn't.
