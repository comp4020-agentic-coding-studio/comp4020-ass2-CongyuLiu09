For most of the twentieth century, a car had one format for almost everything
it needed to tell its driver: a needle, a printed arc, and the driver's own
memory of where that needle sits when everything is fine. Last week's
contrast — the Jaguar E-Type's six analogue dials against the Mercedes MBUX
Hyperscreen's fully digital surface — showed the two ends of a hundred-year
span. This week stays inside the analogue end and asks why it lasted so long,
and what it quietly assumed about the person reading it.

## Continuous state, not just a threshold

A gauge's real value isn't the number it shows at any instant — it's that the
number keeps moving, and a driver can watch it drift. An oil-pressure gauge
lets someone notice pressure easing down over several minutes, long before
any fixed threshold would trip a light. That's diagnostic information a
discrete on/off signal (next week's subject) cannot carry: a slow drift and a
sudden drop look identical to a light that only knows "fine" and "not fine."

## The reader the gauge assumes

That value comes at a cost. A dial prints its danger zone in red, but it
never prints the "normal" zone — the position the needle occupies during
ordinary driving. A driver only knows that position from repetition: a few
weeks in the same car, watching the needle enough times that its rest
position becomes a felt fact rather than a read one. Rent an unfamiliar car
and the redline is legible instantly; the normal cruising position isn't,
because nothing on the dial says so. Analogue gauges, in other words, assume
a driver who drives this car, or one very like it, often enough to have built
that memory beforehand — not a first-time or occasional driver reading it
cold.

An occasional or first-time driver never completes stage 2, which is why a
rental car's tachometer reads as "a needle somewhere in the safe two-thirds"
rather than as useful information.

## Case study: two dashboards, two claims about priority

Porsche has centred the tachometer — not the speedometer — directly ahead of
the driver since the 356 in the 1950s, through every 911 generation since,
including current digital-instrument cars where swapping the priority would
have been trivial. The speedometer sits smaller, to one side. The claim is
specific: in a car whose engine range and gearing the driver is expected to
actively manage, engine load is the number that needs the fastest, least
effortful glance — faster than road speed.

  <img src={tachometerRestPosition.src} alt="A tachometer dial numbered 0 to 8 with a red zone from 6 to 8 and a needle resting near 2, with an arrow labelled 'printed redline' pointing at the red zone and a second arrow labelled 'normal cruise — memorised, not printed' pointing at the needle's rest position" /> 
  <figcaption>Fig. 2.3 — Porsche's own version of the same gap: the redline is on the glass; where the needle should sit while cruising never is.</figcaption>
</figure>

Mainstream family cars make the opposite claim by centring the speedometer
instead — a Toyota Camry, a Honda Accord, a Volkswagen Golf all put road
speed in the largest dial, with the tachometer smaller or reduced to a
digital strip, because the number with legal and safety consequences attached
directly is the one the driver needs fastest, and an automatic transmission
mostly manages engine behaviour on their behalf. Neither layout is simply
correct; each is a design statement about what matters most to the driver in
front of it, encoded before the car ever leaves the factory.
