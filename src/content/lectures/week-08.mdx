Every instrument this course has looked at so far lives below the
windscreen, in a spot the driver has to choose to look at. That choice costs
time — half a second down, half a second back up, at 100 km/h roughly 28
metres travelled blind each way. A head-up display (HUD) stops charging that
toll by putting the instrument in the driver's line of sight, so reading it
and watching the road become the same act. The question isn't "does that
work?" — it plainly does, for speed — but "what else changes once a
manufacturer can draw on the windscreen, and what happens when they draw too
much?"

## Depth, not just position

A gauge cluster's design problem is legibility from a fixed head position at
a fixed distance. A HUD's problem is different: the image has to sit at a
specific *depth* in the driver's field of view, usually projected several
metres ahead so the eye doesn't have to refocus from road to dashboard and
back. Get that focal distance wrong and the refocusing cost the HUD was
meant to remove comes back anyway, just relocated. This is why early HUDs
carried almost nothing — a single number, changing the eye's direction but
not its depth.

## From reporting to overlaying

The larger shift is what a windscreen instrument is allowed to *point at*. A
dashboard gauge only ever refers to itself — a needle means "here's your
speed," full stop. A windscreen graphic can be drawn in registration with a
specific object outside the car: the exact lane, the exact turn a navigation
arrow means. Once a display is conformal to the world, it stops being a
gauge and starts being annotation — a larger claim on attention, and a
larger risk: annotate too much of the road and you've built clutter over the
one view a driver cannot afford to lose.

## Case study: from one number to the whole windscreen

The 1988 Oldsmobile Cutlass Supreme was the first mass-market car sold with
a factory HUD, borrowed openly from fighter-jet avionics, where pilots had
used windscreen-projected instruments since the 1950s to stay eyes-up during
high-speed manoeuvres. Oldsmobile's version was deliberately minimal: a
green, monochrome speed readout reflected off a small combiner, solving one
problem — glancing at the speedometer — and nothing else. Fixed to the car,
not the world, it could report a number but never point at anything.

Modern augmented-reality HUDs, fitted to various current Mercedes-Benz, BMW
and other premium models, do something categorically different: they paint
a navigation arrow onto the actual lane the driver should take, or
highlight a hazard where it sits on the road, updating in real time as the
driver's head shifts. The gain is real — the arrow means "turn here," not
"turn right in 200m." The risk is the risk of any annotation over a live
scene: at what point do a navigation arrow, a hazard highlight and a
following-distance graphic, rendered at once, stop supplementing the road
and start competing with it?

  The HUD, read against the six-question framework introduced in week 1:
  **urgency** it inherits from whatever it displays rather than adding on
  its own; **visibility** that is foveal in position but no longer requires
  refocusing, since the image sits at road depth rather than dashboard
  depth; **meaning** ranging from self-evident (a conformal arrow drawn
  onto the actual lane) to fully learned (a bare speed number); **modality**
  still purely visual, but layered directly over the driving scene instead
  of beside it; **timing** typically continuous while the underlying data
  keeps updating; and a **required action** the conformal graphic can make
  almost automatic ("follow this exact line") in a way a dashboard needle
  never could. The risk the framework doesn't price on its own is
  *competition*: stack enough conformal graphics on the one view a driver
  cannot look away from, and the HUD starts fighting the road for the same
  attention it was built to protect.
</div>
