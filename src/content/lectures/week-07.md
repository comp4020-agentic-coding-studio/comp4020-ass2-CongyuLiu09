Last week the dashboard spoke in chimes; this week it starts writing full
sentences. For most of the instrument cluster's history, text on the dash was
rationed to the smallest unit that would fit on a seven-segment or dot-matrix
display: three letters, sometimes four. A driver who saw `SVS` or `ESP OFF`
light up was handed a code, not a message, and the actual meaning lived in the
owner's manual, in a glovebox, several kilometres away from the moment they
needed it. The multi-information display changed the economics of that
exchange: once the cluster had a real screen instead of a segmented readout,
the car could simply say what it meant. The guiding question this week is
what a sentence buys over an icon or an acronym, and what it costs to buy it.

## A sentence removes ambiguity an icon cannot

An icon is a hypothesis about what the driver will infer, compressed into a
shape small enough to read in peripheral vision. That compression is the
source of its speed and the source of its failure mode: a thermometer icon
over a dashed line means "check coolant temperature," but only if the driver
has already learned that convention, and it means nothing at all to a driver
encountering it for the first time under stress. A sentence closes that gap
by construction. "Low tyre pressure — check tyres" cannot be misread as an
engine problem, cannot be confused with a different icon on a cluttered
cluster, and requires no prior training. Where an icon asks the driver to
supply the missing verb, a sentence supplies it for them.

## What a sentence costs that a glance doesn't

The price is time. An icon is processed by peripheral, largely pre-attentive
vision in a fraction of a second; a sentence requires foveal vision — the eye
has to fixate on the text, and reading is serial, word by word. Human-factors
research on in-vehicle displays consistently finds that reading even a short
message off-road-centre measurably increases eyes-off-road time relative to
recognising a symbol, which is exactly the resource a driver has least of when
something is actually wrong. Comprehension accuracy and reading time trade
against each other: the more precisely a message specifies the fault, the
longer the sentence, and the longer the sentence, the longer the driver's eyes
are off the road to receive it.

## Case study: the acronym, the sentence, and the language it's written in

Take a single fault — a tyre-pressure-monitoring alert. The older pattern
pairs a warning icon with a code the driver has to look up, or with an
acronym like `TPMS` that means nothing without prior exposure. The newer
pattern, on a multi-information display, spells it out: "Low tyre pressure —
check tyres," sometimes naming the specific wheel. This is a genuine
improvement in comprehension. But it introduces a problem the icon never
had. A pictogram of a tyre cross-section with an exclamation mark, per ISO
2575, means the same thing whether the car is sold in Munich, Osaka or
São Paulo — it ships once. A sentence has to be translated into every market
language the vehicle is sold in, and translated well: "check tyres" rendered
too literally, or by a contractor without automotive-domain context, can read
as vague, alarming, or simply wrong in the target language, in ways a
universal pictogram is structurally immune to. Text buys precision and
spends localisation — a cost line that scales with every market the
manufacturer enters, and one that a badly-funded localisation pass can turn
into a new source of the exact ambiguity the sentence was supposed to remove.

## Suggested reference images

- A modern multi-information display showing a plain-language warning such
  as "Low tyre pressure — check tyres," alongside an older cluster showing
  only a warning icon and a two- or three-letter acronym for the same fault
- The same manufacturer's owner's manual warning-message table, showing the
  message translated across two or three languages, to compare phrasing
  length and directness
- A dashboard telltale bank next to its corresponding multi-information
  display text equivalent, to show the icon-plus-sentence pairing in use
