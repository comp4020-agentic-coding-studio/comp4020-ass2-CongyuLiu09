Last week's icons gave the dashboard a vocabulary of shapes — a fuel pump,
a thermometer, a battery. This week asks a narrower and more dangerous
question: before a driver has even parsed the shape, before they've read a
single word, colour has already told them how worried to be. What is that
system, where did it come from, and what happens to a driver for whom the
system's two most important colours are indistinguishable?

## Colour arrives before comprehension

Peripheral vision is fast and coarse. It resolves colour and motion well
before it resolves shape or text, which is exactly why a telltale illuminating
red pulls a driver's eye off the road a beat before they've identified which
telltale it is. This is a feature, not an accident: a dashboard designer is
routing the most time-critical judgement — how urgent is this? — through the
fastest channel the visual system has, and saving the slower channel (shape,
text) for the second question, which is what, specifically, is wrong. Colour
does triage. Icon does diagnosis.

## A borrowed grammar, not an invented one

The SAE J209 / ISO 2575 telltale colour convention — red for immediate
attention or hazard, amber/yellow for advisory or caution, green or blue for
informational or confirmation — was not designed from scratch for cars. It is
lifted, largely intact, from nineteenth-century railway semaphore and
twentieth-century traffic signalling, where the same three-tier logic (stop,
caution, proceed) had already been drilled into an entire population before a
single one of them sat in a car. A dashboard designer specifying a red oil
light in 1970 was not teaching drivers a new code; they were renting one that
already lived in every driver's head from the nearest traffic light.

## The process: from fault to judgement

Colour-coded urgency is the visible tip of a chain that starts well before
the dashboard lights up.

  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">1. Fault detected</span>
    A sensor crosses a threshold — oil pressure drops, wheel speeds disagree.
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">2. Severity classified</span>
    The car maps the fault to one of a small number of tiers.
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">3. Colour assigned</span>
    Critical → red, advisory → amber, informational → green or blue.
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">4. Telltale illuminated</span>
    In that colour, usually alongside its icon.
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">5. Peripheral vision registers</span>
    Colour resolves before foveal vision has identified the icon.
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">6. Urgency judgement made</span>
    Pull over / note it / no action — often before naming the icon.
  </div>
</div>

Steps 1–4 happen inside the car in milliseconds; steps 5–6 happen inside the
driver, and step 5 is the one that fails for a colour-blind driver.

## Case study: the convention, and the population it fails

The convention's strength is exactly its inheritance: no learning curve,
works across languages, works at a glance. Its weakness is that it puts the
entire urgency signal on one channel — hue — and asks that channel to carry a
life-or-death distinction between exactly the two colours, red and green,
hardest to tell apart for the roughly 1 in 12 men (and a much smaller
fraction of women) with some form of colour-blindness, overwhelmingly
red-green deuteranopia or protanopia. For that driver, a red brake-system
warning and a green cruise-control confirmation can render as near-identical
brownish-yellow. The system meant to let them triage without reading anything
now gives no triage information at all — they're thrown straight to reading
icons and text, under the same time pressure, with the fast channel
disabled. The fix already exists in the standard's own margins: SAE and ISO
telltales are also differentiated by shape, fill pattern and cluster
position, redundancy a well-designed dashboard leans on and a cheap one
doesn't bother with. Colour should confirm, not carry, the whole signal.

<div class="dash-panel">
  <span class="dash-panel__label">This week's channel, against the framework</span>

  Colour, read against the six-question framework: it *is* the fastest
  available proxy for **urgency**, delivered through **peripheral**
  visibility rather than foveal — the whole design bet is that it works
  before a driver looks straight at it. Its **meaning** is almost entirely
  pre-learned, borrowed from traffic and rail signalling rather than taught
  by the car. **Modality** is visual only, which is exactly its failure
  point for colour-blind drivers. **Timing** persists for as long as the
  underlying tier holds, and the **required action** it implies is coarse
  but immediate — sort into stop, attend, or ignore, before any icon is
  read.
</div>
