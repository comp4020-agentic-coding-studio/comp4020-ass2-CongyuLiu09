Start with a question rather than a definition: when a dashboard shows you
something, who decided what counts as worth showing, and how much of your
attention it should cost? A fuel gauge and a check-engine light are both
"information," but not the same kind, and a dashboard that treated them
identically would be a bad dashboard. This course studies that difference —
call it a grammar — across a century of instrument design. Week 1 sets the
terms the rest of the course will use.

## A dashboard is an argument, not an inventory

An analogue speedometer needle and a digital speed readout carry the same
number but make different claims. The needle argues by position — its angle
relative to redline is legible at a glance, without reading digits. The digital
number argues by precision — exact, but requiring a half-second of literacy
the needle doesn't. Neither is more "true." Each is a choice about what the
driver needs under a specific kind of pressure: glance-legibility versus
exactness. That choice is the language.

## Constraint is the whole design problem

A dashboard's audience is a driver who is, by definition, mostly looking
somewhere else. Every element competes for a few hundred milliseconds of
peripheral or foveal attention, at speed, often at night, sometimes while
frightened. A museum label can ask for sustained reading; a dashboard cannot.
This is why the course is not a styling exercise — the interesting decisions
are what gets a needle, what gets a light, what gets silence.

## Five channels a dashboard can speak in

Every dashboard element is built from a small set of channels, and the choice
between them is the design decision, not an implementation detail:

- **needle / gauge** — continuous, shows trend and rate of change
- **discrete light (telltale)** — binary, shows a threshold or fault state
- **icon / pictogram** — categorical, names *what*, not *how much*
- **text** — specific, but requires literacy and a moment of reading
- **sound / voice** — hard to miss, but can't be glanced at and dismissed

Later weeks take each of these in turn — week 2 stays entirely inside the
needle, week 3 is the light, week 4 the icon, week 6 the sound. This week's
job is just to notice the choice exists at all.

## How an alert actually happens

A dashboard warning is the visible end of a six-stage process, worth naming
precisely because every later week revisits some stage of it. Most
dashboard-design failures live in the gap between stage 3 and stage 4: a
correctly detected, correctly prioritised event, communicated through the
wrong channel.

<div class="dash-flow">
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">1. Detect</span>
    A sensor crosses a threshold — e.g. a pressure switch drops below a set point
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">2. Interpret</span>
    The vehicle system classifies the event: normal range, a fault, or dangerous?
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">3. Prioritise</span>
    An alert priority is assigned — informational, caution, or warning
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">4. Display</span>
    A channel is chosen: needle movement, light, icon, text, sound, or a mix
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">5. Driver reads</span>
    The driver recognises the alert and judges its urgency
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">6. Driver responds</span>
    Ignore, monitor, or act — pull over, change behaviour
  </div>
</div>

## Case study: Jaguar E-Type (1961) vs Mercedes-Benz MBUX Hyperscreen (2021)

The Series 1 E-Type's dashboard is two large matching circular dials —
speedometer and tachometer — flanked by smaller gauges for oil pressure,
water temperature, fuel and battery condition, all analogue, all continuous
needles, set in engine-turned aluminium. There is no telltale light bank at
all in the earliest cars: a fault shows up as a needle drifting into a
marked danger zone, not as a discrete on/off signal. The driver's job is
continuous monitoring — reading trends, not waiting for alarms.

<figure class="dash-figure">
  <img src={eTypeNoTelltales.src} alt="Diagram of a 1961 Jaguar E-Type dashboard showing twin matching speedometer and tachometer dials, four smaller gauges, and toggle switches, with a dashed circle around the empty space where a modern telltale array would sit, labelled 'nothing here in 1961'" />
  <figcaption>Fig. 1.3 — No idiot lights: every fault reads as a needle in the wrong place.</figcaption>
</figure>

The MBUX Hyperscreen, by contrast, is a single curved glass panel spanning
the width of the car, housing three displays (driver cluster, centre, and
front-passenger screen) behind one seamless surface. Almost every reading —
speed, range, climate, navigation, drive mode — is a software-rendered
surface that can be reconfigured, restyled or replaced by an update; the
instrument a Hyperscreen driver reads on Tuesday may not be laid out the same
way on Wednesday.

<figure class="dash-figure">
  <img src={reconfigurableCluster.src} alt="Two identical screen frames side by side: the left showing a gauge-priority layout with a large speedometer dial, the right showing the same panel in navigation-priority mode with a route arrow, bracketed together" />
  <figcaption>Fig. 1.4 — The instrument cluster as software: the same panel of glass can be a speedometer or a map, decided at runtime rather than at the factory.</figcaption>
</figure>

Same four questions — how fast, how much fuel, how hot, which way — answered
by opposite grammars: one fixed, mechanical and non-negotiable; the other
soft, configurable, mediated by software that can change the answer's shape
without changing the car underneath it. Neither is free: the E-Type cannot
warn you of anything its designers didn't wire a gauge for; the Hyperscreen
can show you almost anything, which means someone has to keep deciding what
it shouldn't.

<div class="dash-panel">
  <span class="dash-panel__label">This week's channels, against the framework</span>

  Every channel this course studies can be read against the same six
  questions — introduced in full here, and returned to every week from now
  on for that week's specific channel:

  - **Urgency** — how much time pressure is the driver actually under?
  - **Visibility** — does this sit in foveal (direct) or peripheral vision?
  - **Meaning** — is it self-explanatory, or does it need to be learned?
  - **Modality** — sight, hearing, or touch?
  - **Timing** — does it fire once, persist, escalate, or repeat?
  - **Required driver action** — what, concretely, should the driver do?

  A fuel gauge, read against these six: low urgency most of the time
  (escalating only near empty), foveal, self-explanatory once the E–F scale
  is known, visual, persistent for the whole drive, and requires no action
  until the trend gets close to the red.
</div>
