Last week's colour grammar assumed the driver is looking at the dashboard.
Sound doesn't, which is exactly why it's harder to get right. A red
telltale only works on someone already looking toward it; a chime reaches
someone looking at the road, the mirror, a child arguing in the back seat.
That reach is the whole appeal of the sonic register, and its whole risk —
a sound that doesn't need your eyes also doesn't stop when you'd like it
to, and a driver who has learned to tune one out has lost the one channel
built to work regardless of where they were looking.

## Urgency is not loudness

The easy mistake is to treat volume as a proxy for importance: louder means
more urgent. It doesn't, reliably. A car can be quiet about something
critical (a single low chime for a cracked windscreen sensor fault) and
insistent about something merely inconvenient (a door held slightly ajar).
Loudness is tuned mainly for audibility over engine and road noise, not for
communicating rank among warnings — so something else has to carry urgency.

## Four things a designer can actually turn

That something else is a small set of independently adjustable variables:
pitch (higher reads as more pressing, up to a point where it starts
reading as a fault rather than a warning), tempo (a faster repeat rate
reads as closer or more immediate — a parking sensor's beep speeding up as
the gap closes is the cleanest example on the dashboard), repetition (does
it sound once, reporting an event, or keep returning, reporting a
condition that persists), and timbre (a tone, a recorded voice, and a
melodic "earcon" carry different weight at matched volume). None of these
is fixed by physics. Each is a decision, legible to any driver who has
heard enough cars to compare them, even without the words to say why one
chime feels more frantic than another at the same volume.

## Process: how a warning sound actually gets deployed

A chime doesn't just "go off" — it moves through a pipeline, and how far it
is allowed to escalate at the last stage is a policy decision, not a
technical one.

<div class="dash-flow">
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">1. Event detected</span>
    Sensor reports an unbuckled belt, a closing gap, a lane departure.
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">2. Tier assigned</span>
    The event is classified against a severity scale fixed at development.
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">3. Palette queried</span>
    The tier maps to a specific sound from the manufacturer's library.
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">4. Sound played</span>
    At the volume, pitch and repetition rate that tier specifies.
  </div>
  <div class="dash-flow__step">
    <span class="dash-flow__step-label">5. Response monitored</span>
    If the condition doesn't clear, the system may escalate — louder,
    faster, or an added channel such as haptic seat buzz.
  </div>
</div>

## Case study: the seatbelt chime's escalation, and Tesla's refusal of it

Regulatory testing regimes have spent two decades pushing the seatbelt
chime in one direction: louder, more repetitive, harder to ignore.
Protocols used by NHTSA's New Car Assessment Program and Euro NCAP's Safety
Assist criteria award manufacturers credit for reminders that escalate —
not a polite ding, but a chime that gets louder and more frequent, paired
with a flashing telltale, the longer the belt stays unbuckled while the car
moves. The logic is defensible: compliance measurably improves when the
nag gets worse, so testing regimes reward designs that make ignoring the
belt actively unpleasant. The result is a chime engineered to be annoying
by design brief, not by accident.

Tesla takes the opposite bet. The seatbelt chime still exists — it's
federally mandated, not optional — but Tesla generally doesn't layer the
escalating loop many competitors add once that legal minimum has played.
Status that would be an ongoing sound elsewhere becomes a persistent but
silent icon on the touchscreen instead. Where the escalating chime assumes
a driver who needs discomfort to comply, the minimal set assumes a driver
who, told once, doesn't need telling again louder. Neither assumption is
obviously wrong; they're different bets about how much trust a warning
system extends to the person it's warning.

  Sound, read against the six-question framework: **urgency** is the one
  variable it can express through several independent dials at once —
  pitch, tempo, repetition — rather than colour's single hue. Its defining
  property is **visibility**: it needs none, reaching a driver whose eyes
  are anywhere at all. **Meaning** is a mix — tempo-as-distance is read
  almost universally, but which chime maps to which fault is still learned.
  **Modality** is auditory. **Timing** is this week's real axis, from a
  single reporting chime to an escalating, repeating one. And the
  **required action** a sound implies scales with how far a system is
  allowed to escalate before the driver responds.
