## Before the studio

Bring one real driver-assistance alert you've personally experienced or can
find clearly documented — a forward-collision warning, a lane-keep-assist
correction nudging the wheel, an adaptive-cruise-control gap alert, an
automatic-emergency-braking activation, anything from an owner's manual,
video, or your own car.

## In the studio

Idris hands out a shared list of a dozen real alerts pooled from what
students brought in. In small groups, sort every alert on the list into one
of two piles: "reports a state" (tells the driver a fact and leaves the
decision to them) or "narrates a decision" (describes an action the car is
taking, or about to take, on its own). Several alerts are written to sit
uncomfortably in the middle — a lane-keep-assist correction, for instance, is
a small autonomous action but a barely-perceptible one, and groups usually
split on it. Once every group has sorted the full list, compare piles across
the room and dig into the disagreements: what made an alert ambiguous, and
was the ambiguity in the alert's design or in the group's definitions?

## Afterwards

Write up the one alert your group argued over longest and the category you
concluded is harder to design well — a report that must be precise, or a
narration that must not feel like a malfunction. This becomes your worked
example if the mini knowledge check in the week 9 deck calls on it.
