## Before the studio

No preparation required, but come having actually driven or ridden in a car
with a lane-keeping or collision-warning feature at some point — a rental, a
family car, a rideshare. If you haven't, think of the noisiest notification
system on your phone instead.

## In the studio

Idris runs a quick thought experiment individually first: for a
lane-departure warning and, separately, a forward-collision warning, each
student writes down the false-alarm rate at which they'd personally start
ignoring the alert, and the rate at which they'd go further and disable it
entirely — "1 in 3 chimes wrong" versus "1 in 20." Answers go on the board
under both alert names. The spread is usually wide, and that's the point:
the room then compares numbers and argues about why — does it depend on
how the alert interrupts (a chime versus a seat vibration versus a full
lane-tug), on what it costs to silence, or on how bad a missed real event
would be. The session closes by asking whether a single manufacturer-chosen
threshold can ever be right for a room this split.

## Afterwards

Keep your written thresholds — they're a useful personal data point for
arguing, later in the course, about whether a fixed regulatory threshold can
ever fit every driver equally well.
